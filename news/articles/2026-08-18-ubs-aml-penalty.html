Record-Setting AML Penalty Against UBS

FinCEN and three other regulators levied a combined $125 million penalty on UBS Financial Services for AML failures that went back more than a decade.

Source: Compliance Week

What happened

FinCEN and three other federal regulators levied a combined $125 million penalty against UBS Financial Services (UBSFS), a broker-dealer and futures commission merchant, for a series of long-standing anti-money laundering failures. FinCEN and the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency accounted for $77 million of the total, with FINRA, the SEC, and the CFTC contributing $20 million, $20 million, and $8 million respectively.

The case goes back years. UBSFS entered a consent order with FinCEN in 2018 and paid a $14.5 million penalty for AML failures, representing that it would fix its transaction monitoring system for foreign currency wires by mid-2019. The legacy system was screening transactions infrequently, at best quarterly, and relied on an error-prone Excel-based process that had existed since 2004. Senior executives knew within weeks of the 2018 order that the mid-2019 deadline would be missed, but that was not relayed to the government. A modern automated monitoring system only launched in 2021, and even then remained defective well into 2023, with over 5 percent of foreign currency wires still unmonitored. FinCEN found UBSFS ultimately processed over $10.5 billion in wires without sufficient monitoring.

FinCEN also found customer due diligence failures: the firm did not properly consider source of wealth, negative news, politically exposed person status, or account restrictions. One example involved a Russian oligarch linked in press coverage to Vladimir Putin, who was permitted to maintain multiple accounts and move millions in transfers despite restrictions.

Why this is a GRC story

This is a masterclass in the cost of recidivism and slow remediation. The first penalty in 2018 did not produce lasting change, and the second penalty is roughly nine times larger. Regulators do not forget the prior consent order, and the pattern here is textbook: a remediation deadline set and missed, executives aware of the miss, and a fix that arrived late and incomplete.

The second lesson is about monitoring systems as a control. A transaction monitoring system is not a project, it is a control, and it only works if the data feeds are complete. UBSFS's new system existed but was not properly receiving data, which is a failure mode every compliance team should recognize. Building a control and operating it are different jobs.

The third lesson is enforcement posture. FinCEN collected roughly $37 million from broker-dealers in total before this year, and has levied $205 million in such penalties year-to-date. Add the new FinCEN whistleblower program, and firms with unresolved prior findings have more reason than ever to take remediation seriously.

What to watch

Watch whether other broker-dealers and financial institutions accelerate AML remediation, and watch the whistleblower pipeline for new cases. For any compliance program, the practical takeaway is to treat every regulator commitment as a hard deadline, keep evidence that remediation actually happened, and audit your data feeds before you audit your alerts.
