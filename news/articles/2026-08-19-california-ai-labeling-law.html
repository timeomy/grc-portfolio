California AI Labeling Law Takes Effect With Detection Tools Missing

California's AI Transparency Act now requires AI disclosure tools, but an audit found several major developers have no public detector yet.

Source: Compliance Week

What happened

California's AI Transparency Act took effect on August 2, making it the first state to enforce content-labeling rules for AI-generated material. The law applies to major generative AI developers, including OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Microsoft. It requires them to give users a detection tool and to attach machine-readable, and in many cases visible, disclosures marking content as AI-generated or altered.

An investigation by The Indicator, working with the digital rights group WITNESS, checked 13 companies against those obligations. Seven of them reportedly had no dedicated public detection tool available, which looks like a direct violation of the new California requirements and of the EU AI Act, whose transparency rules also took effect on August 2. Of the companies that did offer a detector, only one correctly identified every image produced by its own AI tool.

Why this is a GRC story

This is the moment a law meets reality, and the reality is messy. Writing a rule is easy. Building the detection technology the rule assumes, then shipping it to users, is the hard part. The gap here is not bad intent, it is operational readiness: the obligations started on a fixed date and the tooling did not arrive with them.

For compliance teams this is a useful reminder that regulators increasingly write requirements that depend on technology maturity, and they do not always wait for the technology to catch up. If your organization is subject to a disclosure or labeling regime, the practical question is not whether the law is fair. It is whether you can demonstrate compliance with evidence when the attorney general comes calling. California enforcement sits with the state attorney general, city attorneys and county counsel, so the enforcers are numerous and local.

The compliance angle also extends beyond California. Washington state plans similar requirements starting in January, with Oregon and New York expected to follow, and California's law will expand into a second phase requiring social media platforms to embed AI markers into posted content. Compliance officers should treat California's rules as a floor, not a ceiling, because the direction of travel across US states is now clear.

What to watch

Watch whether the missing detection tools appear quietly in the coming weeks or force the first enforcement action. Also watch how the detection gap is framed: if regulators accept that the tools are coming, the story stays technical. If they treat August 2 as a hard deadline, the first fines will set the precedent for every state that copies California's approach.
