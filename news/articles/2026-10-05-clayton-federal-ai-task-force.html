Trump Taps Jay Clayton to Lead a Federal AI Task Force

The White House has put its intelligence chief in charge of a new federal AI task force, weeks after AI firms signed a voluntary safety accord.

Source: SecurityWeek

What happened

President Donald Trump has named Jay Clayton, his director of national intelligence, to lead a new government task force on artificial intelligence. Trump calls it the Super Intelligence Force, part of an effort to rebrand advanced AI as super intelligence. The announcement came days after he hosted the chief executives of major AI companies at the White House and said they had signed a voluntary accord to self-police the development of the technology.

The task force is described as coordinating the federal effort to keep the United States ahead in the field. In a post on social media, Trump said it will reach out to consumers, public interest groups, religious organizations, critical infrastructure providers and AI companies.

Others named to the group include Andrew Ferguson, chairman of the Federal Trade Commission, Emil Michael, the Pentagon's chief technology officer, and Scott Kupor, director of the Office of Personnel Management. The task force reports to Trump and his chief of staff, Susie Wiles.

Why this is a GRC story

Coordination is now the stated control. When a government has no single AI regulator, a task force is the mechanism it reaches for instead. For anyone building an AI governance programme, that is worth reading as a signal about where expectations will come from, which is a mix of agencies rather than one rulebook.

The regulator is inside the room. The FTC chairman sits on a body that also has investigative and enforcement powers. The same agency reported to be investigating frontier AI companies is now helping to set the direction of federal AI work, and that overlap is the thing to watch.

Voluntary first, mandatory later. The accord signed with the companies says it may over time make sense to codify these steps into laws or regulations. That sentence matters more than the ceremony around it. Voluntary frameworks usually become the draft text of something compulsory, so the controls named now are the ones likely to be examined later.

Critical infrastructure providers are on the outreach list. If suppliers and operators of essential services are asked to show how they govern AI, that request travels down the supply chain and lands on procurement and vendor risk teams.

What to watch

Watch whether the task force produces anything with teeth: deadlines, reporting duties, or a named set of controls. A coordination body with no authority still shapes expectations, but it does not bind anyone.

Watch the FTC investigation as it develops. If agentic or frontier AI failures get defined as unfair or deceptive practices, the question of who owns AI risk shifts from policy debate to enforcement, and that is the point at which a risk register has to name an owner.
