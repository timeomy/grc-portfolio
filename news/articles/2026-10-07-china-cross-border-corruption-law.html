China's Draft Anti-Cross-Border Corruption Law Would Make Compliance Programs Mandatory

China's first draft law on cross-border corruption, now in public comment, would move bribery compliance from voluntary practice to legal obligation.

Source: Compliance Week

What happened

China's draft Anti-Cross-Border Corruption Law is moving through the National People's Congress, and Compliance Week reports that it adds a compliance burden on foreign companies. The bill was submitted for first reading on August 25, 2026 at the 24th session of the Standing Committee of the 14th NPC, proposed by the National Commission of Supervision, and was published for public comment on August 28. The comment period closed on September 26.

The draft runs to six chapters and 47 articles. It is China's first piece of draft legislation aimed specifically at cross-border corruption, covering both domestic companies that bribe foreign officials and foreign companies that bribe officials inside China. The chapters cover general provisions, duties and mechanisms, case handling and international cooperation, integrity compliance, legal liability, and supplementary provisions.

A review by AllBright Law Offices notes that the legal liability chapter creates no new offenses and sets no fine ranges. The firm's reading is that deterrence still rests on the Criminal Law and other statutes, while this bill is really about who it reaches, how procedures work, how leniency is granted, and how it defends Chinese interests. AllBright places it alongside the Anti-Foreign Sanctions Law and the MOFCOM rules that block the unjustified application of foreign legislation, a framework it describes as blocking and countermeasures. Its conclusion for companies operating abroad is direct: compliance obligations are shifting from voluntary to mandatory, and systems should be built early.

Why this is a GRC story

Compliance stops being optional. Anti-bribery programs at many firms are voluntary undertakings modeled on US and UK expectations. A law that reaches the same conduct from the Chinese side makes those programs a legal requirement rather than good practice, and a program that only exists on paper will not satisfy it.

Two regulators now pull in opposite directions. A company subject to US enforcement rules and to this draft faces competing duties on disclosure and on cooperating with foreign probes. Third party due diligence, books and records, and how a firm responds to a government request all become contested ground. Compliance leaders should map where the two regimes collide rather than assume one governs.

Leniency design shapes behavior. How a law rewards self-reporting and remediation determines whether problems surface internally or first through an investigator. Reading the leniency provisions closely is worth as much as reading the prohibitions.

What to watch

Watch the final text after public comment, especially the revised scope of regulated subjects and the leniency mechanism, since first drafts rarely survive intact. Watch whether the bill gives companies clearer expectations for internal compliance programs, in the way deferred prosecution agreements have shaped guidance elsewhere.

Companies with operations or partners in China have a reasonable amount of lead time. The work that ages well is unglamorous: know your third parties, keep records of what you paid and why, and write down the controls that already exist. A compliance program assembled after enforcement begins is worth far less.
