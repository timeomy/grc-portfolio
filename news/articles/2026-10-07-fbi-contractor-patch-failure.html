FBI Removes Accenture Contractor After an Unpatched Platform Led to a Data Breach

The FBI removed a third party contractor after a security patch went uninstalled, opening the platform ShinyHunters used to steal bureau employees' data.

Source: The Hacker News

What happened

The FBI has removed an Accenture contractor from its work after a breach that exposed the personal details of thousands of bureau employees, according to reporting by Reuters that cited two people familiar with the matter. ShinyHunters, the group behind the intrusion, said it took the data through the FBI's job portal in September.

Brett Leatherman, assistant director of the FBI's cyber division, told Reuters that the incident resulted from a "security failure of a platform managed by a third-party organization" after a contractor failed to implement a security patch that had been explicitly issued to secure that platform. He said the bureau removed the contractor and took steps to mitigate further risk and protect its workforce.

The FBI did not name the third party organization. Reuters reported it is Oracle PeopleSoft, the platform ShinyHunters said it exploited. Security firm Mandiant assessed that the group bypassed a web application firewall rule blocking a vulnerable endpoint using a URL encoding trick, tied to CVE-2026-35273. Accenture said in a statement that it is proud to support the FBI's mission and will continue to do so. The FBI has said two members of ShinyHunters have been arrested and that more arrests are likely.

Why this is a GRC story

A patch is only a control once it is deployed. The public account describes a fix that existed and was never applied. In most organizations the weakness is not knowing about the vulnerability, it is proving that remediation actually happened on the system that matters. A vulnerability register with a status field is not evidence. Patch confirmation from the platform owner is.

Third party contracts carry security duties that rarely get tested. The platform was managed by an outside party, and the failure sat with that party's execution. That is exactly the exposure vendor risk programs are meant to catch. Contracts that require patching within a set window are worth little without a way to verify compliance, and without a remedy that a security team can actually invoke when the window is missed.

Compensating controls are not a substitute for a fix. The intrusion reportedly involved getting past a firewall rule guarding the vulnerable endpoint. A WAF in front of an unpatched system buys time, and time only helps if the underlying patch lands. Treating the firewall as the fix is where governance quietly fails.

What to watch

Watch how the FBI characterizes contractor accountability in its next public statements, because a federal agency naming a vendor's failure sets a tone other buyers may follow. Watch, too, for whether the PeopleSoft exposure prompts wider patching reviews at other agencies running the same platform.

The practical lesson is unglamorous. For every externally managed system that holds sensitive data, confirm a named owner, a defined patching window, and a way to verify that patches land. Then test one of those systems this quarter rather than assuming the contract did the work.
