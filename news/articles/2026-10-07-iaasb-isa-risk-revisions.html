IAASB Opens Consultation on Revisions to Three Core Auditing Standards

The IAASB is taking comments until December 15 on proposed changes to ISA 330, ISA 500 and ISA 520 covering audit evidence and risk response.

Source: Compliance Week

What happened

The International Auditing and Assurance Standards Board has opened a consultation on revisions to three core International Standards on Auditing that deal with audit evidence and how auditors respond to assessed risks. Compliance Week reports that the board is calling for responses from anyone involved in producing or using financial statements, or otherwise involved in corporate governance, including practitioners, academics and investors.

The three standards under review are ISA 330, The Auditor's Responses to Assessed Risks; ISA 500, Audit Evidence; and ISA 520, Analytical Procedures. The IAASB says the proposals are meant to complete an important phase of its long running program to modernize the standards. The board describes two goals: to further embed a principles based, risk based framework, and to support digital innovation without writing rules that are tied to specific technologies that will age quickly.

The board has published exposure drafts, a response form, and a shorter survey for investors, analysts, lenders and other users of audited financial statements. Both the response form and the user survey are open until December 15, 2026. The proposals build on earlier revisions, including ISA 315 as revised in 2019, and are meant to reinforce professional skepticism and professional judgment throughout an audit. The IAASB is also running a two part webinar series on the proposals.

Why this is a GRC story

Audit standards shape what assurance means. Governance and risk programs lean on the audit as an independent check. When the standards that define evidence, risk response and analytical procedures change, they change what an audit committee can reasonably rely on, and what an internal function needs to produce for an external auditor to reach a conclusion.

Principles beat technology specific rules. The board is deliberately keeping the framework technology agnostic while acknowledging that systems, data and how they are used keep changing. That is the right instinct. Rules written around a particular tool become obsolete before the next revision cycle, whereas a risk based principle can absorb new systems if the reasoning is sound.

Consultations are governance work, not background noise. Standards are more responsive when the people who live with them comment. GRC teams that wait for the final text to arrive for implementation lose the chance to shape the obligations they will later be measured against.

What to watch

Watch how the board treats the use of technology in the evidence and analytics standards, since that is where the tension between principle and practice sits. Watch, too, the timeline after the comment period closes, because implementation dates cascade into audit methodology updates, training and internal control documentation.

If the revisions touch the work your organization already does, the useful step now is small: read the exposure drafts against your current evidence practices and decide whether responding is worth an hour. Frameworks improve when the practitioners who use them take the time to say what will and will not work.
