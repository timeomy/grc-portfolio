Lab Tests Put AI Agents Inside Industrial Control Networks in Minutes

A Booz Allen lab exercise found frontier models completed all eight autonomous OT attack scenarios, including finding and moving a robotic arm.

Source: The Register

What happened

Booz Allen Hamilton tested how far autonomous AI agents could get inside an operational technology environment, and reported that the models achieved the objective in all eight scenarios. The work was done in the firm's OT lab, built to mirror systems the consultants say they see across industries rather than a simplified diagram of one.

The lab was modelled on a general manufacturing facility, with a layered network split into enterprise, industrial DMZ, plant operations and production zones. It contained programmable logic controllers, human machine interfaces, operator workstations, a SCADA platform, a variable frequency drive, a robotic arm and sensors. The models were not identified publicly; Booz Allen described them as frontier models from leading AI providers.

In one scenario the agents found and moved a robotic arm within minutes. In another they went from a perimeter compromise to taking actions inside the industrial control network in just over 16 minutes. Agents were also able to make operator screens on OT devices show something other than reality. Kyle Miller, vice president of infrastructure cybersecurity at Booz Allen, said the testing showed AI agents can operate with speed, persistence and engineering level precision that may outpace organisations which have not implemented foundational OT cybersecurity practices.

Why this is a GRC story

The response window is now shorter than most governance cycles. A 16 minute path from perimeter to control actions sits inside the time it takes most organisations to acknowledge a page, open a bridge and agree who is incident commander. Detection and containment have to be designed for minutes, and that is an investment decision that lands on the risk register whether or not anyone writes it down.

Autonomy plus physical consequence is the AI risk that matters. Most AI governance work of the past two years focused on data, models and acceptable use. This is the version where an agent's actions move equipment and degrade a service people depend on. The questions change: what authority an agent holds, who approves it, how its actions are logged, how a human intervenes, and what evidence exists that the intervention works.

Operator displays are a trust anchor that can be undermined. Engineers act on what the HMI tells them. If an agentic tool can alter what a screen shows while the underlying process state differs, manual observation stops being a compensating control, and that assumption is written into a lot of safety and availability planning.

Foundational practice is still the mitigant. The finding is not that defenders need exotic tooling. It is that segmentation, asset inventory, least privilege, logging and tested response plans are what an autonomous adversary exploits when they are missing.

What to watch

Watch whether the methodology is published in enough detail to reproduce. Vendor lab findings carry marketing risk, and the useful signal will be whether independent researchers or national agencies run comparable tests on real control systems. Watch also how regulators treat agent authority in critical infrastructure.

For anyone responsible for OT risk, the near term action is a tabletop with one change: assume the attacker's decision loop is faster than yours. If your incident response plan depends on a person noticing an anomaly, investigating it and calling someone, that plan is the control that needs testing first.
