Insider Who Held the Domain Controller Gets 32 Months for a Fake Ransom Attack

A former infrastructure engineer at a New Jersey industrial firm planted scheduled tasks that locked out thousands of accounts, then demanded 20 bitcoin.

Source: SecurityWeek

What happened

Daniel Rhyne, a 59 year old Kansas City man, was sentenced to 32 months in federal prison on September 28, 2026 for an extortion plot against his own employer. He had pleaded guilty to extortion in relation to a threat to cause damage to a protected computer, and to intentional damage to a protected computer. At the time he was a core infrastructure engineer at an industrial firm headquartered in Somerset County, New Jersey, serving industries that include biopharmaceuticals, food and beverage, healthcare, manufacturing, oil and gas and metals.

In November 2023 he placed scheduled tasks on the firm's domain controller. Those tasks would delete 13 domain administrator accounts, change passwords for 301 domain user accounts, change passwords for two local administrator accounts affecting 254 servers, and change passwords for two local administrator accounts affecting 3,284 workstations. Where passwords were changed, they were generally set to "TheFr0zenCrew!".

The tasks ran late in the afternoon of November 25. Within an hour, employees received an email from an external address headed "Your Network Has Been Penetrated", warning that administrators had been locked out or deleted, that all backups were gone, and that 40 random servers would shut down each day for ten days unless a ransom was paid. The demand was 20 bitcoin, roughly $750,000 at the time.

There is no suggestion the company paid. It began its own forensic analysis of network anomalies, correlated internal logs with physical access records, and brought in the FBI, which traced the activity to Rhyne's residential IP address in Warren County, New Jersey. He was arrested in August 2024 and pleaded guilty in April 2026 in federal court in Trenton.

Why this is a GRC story

The attack used the ordinary tools of administration. Scheduled tasks, account deletions and bulk password resets are things a core infrastructure engineer does legitimately every week. Nothing sophisticated was required. What catches this is not a malware signature but a change record where the approver differs from the requester, plus an alert when someone runs destructive tasks in bulk outside a change window.

Physical access records carried the case. What turned suspicion into a criminal complaint was correlating system logs with badge data. Many organisations keep those datasets in separate systems owned by separate teams with no shared identifier, which is the gap that makes insider incidents slow and expensive to prove.

Privilege concentration is a design decision with a risk cost. One engineer with standing rights over domain controllers, server local administrators and thousands of workstations is a choice made for operational convenience. It is worth writing down who holds that access today, whether it is still justified, and what would break if it were reduced to just-in-time approval.

What to watch

Watch how the firm describes its remediation in any future statement, particularly whether standing domain rights were narrowed. A 32 month sentence for a first time offender who never deployed ransomware is also a useful data point for anyone arguing for insider threat budget.

Two questions are worth putting to your own environment. If a privileged account created a scheduled task on a weekend afternoon, which alert fires and who receives it? And when did someone last review the list of accounts that can reset a domain administrator password?
