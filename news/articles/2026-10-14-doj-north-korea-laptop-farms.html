DOJ Charges 14 in North Korean Remote IT Worker Scheme

The Justice Department charged 14 people over a North Korean remote IT worker scheme that placed fake employees inside more than 70 US companies.

Source: CyberScoop

What happened

The US Department of Justice has charged 14 people and seized roughly 187 computers in its latest effort to break up North Korea's remote IT worker scheme. An indictment unsealed in the Northern District of Ohio names two US citizens, Michael James Bostelle of Pennsylvania and Juan Cecilio Pena Carrillo of Texas, along with 10 others located in China. Prosecutors say the group held remote jobs at more than 70 companies between 2019 and 2025 using stolen or fabricated identities and collected about $3 million in pay.

Two people were arrested and agents searched more than 40 residences in Texas known or suspected to operate as laptop farms, where facilitators keep employer-issued machines online so overseas workers can use them. An Ohio resident, Trey Michael Nolte, is named as a co-conspirator but not charged. Nolte is accused of keeping roughly two dozen company laptops online, installing remote access software and moving more than $320,000 through accounts opened under false names. Bostelle allegedly supplied his name and a fraudulent driver's licence so a remote worker could be hired, while Carrillo is accused of hosting laptops under various names for about $190,000 from five employers. Prosecutors also allege false employment records were filed with the Department of Homeland Security, the IRS and the Social Security Administration in the names of more than 25 identity theft victims.

The FBI published an updated advisory describing how the scheme has adapted. Under pressure from better detection, North Korean workers now pay US citizens to appear in interviews, take drug tests, access accounts and keep company laptops running at home, and they target people under financial strain. Some hire freelancers in Pakistan, India and Nigeria to do the work itself. CrowdStrike, which assisted investigators, said the group has spoofed device identifiers since May, including presenting a video capture card as a webcam and reusing the same disguised device across several jobs. The State Department is offering rewards of up to $5 million for information that disrupts the scheme.

Why this is a GRC story

Identity assurance is a control problem, not a paperwork problem. The scheme worked because hiring controls stopped at a résumé, a video call and a background check run on a stolen identity. Verified identity at onboarding, device attestation and re-verification during employment separate a compliant hire from a sanctioned state actor on payroll.

Sanctions exposure arrives through payroll. Wages paid to a North Korean worker are funds flowing to a sanctioned state, and the indictment links the scheme to funding weapons programmes. Employers, payroll providers and banks all sit on that flow.

Third party and insider risk converge here. The facilitators were US citizens, recruiters and contractors, not distant intruders. That is the profile most vendor risk programmes underweight.

What to watch

Watch how the FBI's layered checks advice becomes policy, because the controls that catch an imposter can also slow down legitimate remote candidates. Expect more guidance on device fingerprinting and interview verification, and procurement questions about how vendors verify who works on an account.

The practical test for most organisations is simple: if a manager had to prove today that a fully remote engineer is who they claim to be, what evidence could they produce.
