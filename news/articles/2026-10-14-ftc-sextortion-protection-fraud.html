FTC Moves to Shut Down Sellers of Fake Sextortion Protection

The FTC is seeking to shut down 12 companies it says sold sextortion victims thousands of dollars of forensics and monitoring services never delivered.

Source: CyberScoop

What happened

The Federal Trade Commission has asked a federal court in Ohio to stop Dmitry Belkin and 12 of his companies from operating, alleging unfair and deceptive trade practices under the Federal Trade Act and violations of the Telemarketing Act's ban on deceptive and abusive telemarketing since at least 2020.

Most of the businesses, which include Digital Forensics Corp., 1st All Data Recovery and Remote Backup Corp., advertised help for people caught in romance and sextortion scams, where a criminal threatens to publish an intimate image unless the victim pays. They ran sites such as BlackmailHelpline.com and DigitalForensics.com, promoted through search and social advertising to people actively looking for help.

The FTC says the businesses took in at least $60 million while routinely failing to deliver what they sold. Customers were pitched a Phase 1 package costing between $1,499 and $20,000 that promised to stop an ongoing attack, handle communications with the extortionist, trace their location, send a legal cease and desist letter and report evidence to law enforcement. According to the complaint, staff used the customer's own account to message the scammer, changed settings to block them, fabricated chat transcripts and used pre-saved geolocation coordinates to make it look like work had been done. Customers were then pressured into buying Phase 2 monitoring that the FTC says was never provided either.

The complaint describes a telemarketing script built to exploit fear, with employees confirming after calls that they had used phrases such as "pedophile", "devastating consequences", "life altering" and "no coming back after this". The FTC alleges some customers were asked whether their marriages were "on the rocks" or whether they had young children who could "mean a custody battle". Refund requests were routinely denied, complaints were funnelled to a fake Better Business Bureau site controlled by the businesses, and positive reviews were posted by hired drivers using staged photographs. Belkin's attorney did not respond to a request for comment.

Why this is a GRC story

Trust was the product, and it was manufactured. This is not a technology failure. The fake reviews, the fabricating of transcripts and a cloned complaint channel point to a business built on defeating the checks that customers rely on when choosing a vendor under stress.

Victim facing services need an evidence trail. Any firm selling incident response, data removal or extortion negotiation should be able to show what was actually done for a client. Where deliverables cannot be evidenced, both consumer protection and professional liability exposure follow.

Marketing claims are regulated. Statements about coordinating with law enforcement or preventing publication are exactly the claims regulators test first, and the telemarketing counts matter as much as the deception allegations.

What to watch

Watch the injunction ruling and whether the FTC pursues individual owners of other recovery vendors that advertise against panic searches. Firms that refer clients to such vendors should revisit their diligence, because a referral carries an implicit endorsement.
