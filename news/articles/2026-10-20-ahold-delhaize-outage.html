Ahold Delhaize Chose to Take the Systems Down, and Called It the Right Call

Ahold Delhaize confirmed a cyberattack led it to pull IT systems across most US and European brands, its second major incident in two years.

Source: The Register

What happened

Ahold Delhaize, the Dutch grocery group behind Food Lion, Giant Food, Stop & Shop, Hannaford, Albert Heijn and dozens of other brands, confirmed on Monday that a cyberattack led it to take IT systems offline across most of its European and American businesses. The company first disclosed a "security issue within its network" on October 12. By Monday it was calling the cause "malicious actions of others."

The decision to disconnect was deliberate and, by the company's account, correct. "Taking systems offline to help protect them was a precautionary measure that affected most of our brands across Europe and the United States," said president and CEO Frans Muller. "The investigation to date confirms this was the right decision."

All physical stores stayed open. The company said the investigation has not found evidence that sensitive customer, associate or partner data was stolen, which is the sentence every retailer hopes to write and few can write this early. Websites and apps across most brands were pulled. Albert Heijn's site and app are back and home deliveries were expected to resume on Friday, Belgian e commerce is fully operational, and e commerce for the American chains is expected back "in the coming days."

Ahold Delhaize notified the Dutch National Cyber Security Centre last week and filed a report with Dutch police on Monday. The Register asked whether the incident is connected to attacks on refrigeration controllers at hundreds of French supermarkets from October 11, which reportedly pushed temperatures toward 50 degrees Celsius and spoiled food and caused fires. The company did not answer.

This is the second time in two years. A November 2024 intrusion disrupted brands across the group, INC Ransom claimed it and leaked documents, and the company eventually determined that more than 2.2 million people's personal, financial and health data had been compromised.

Why this is a GRC story

Someone had to authorise the outage. Taking ordering, payment and delivery systems offline costs real money every hour. That decision needs a named authority, a documented trigger and a way to reverse it, agreed before the incident, not negotiated during it. Muller's statement is the kind of thing a board can defend only if the paperwork behind it already existed.

Two incidents in two years is a finding, not bad luck. The 2024 intrusion produced a remediation programme. Whatever that programme covered, it did not stop a second event with a similar operational signature. Post incident reviews that close on "we patched the entry point" tend to miss the structural question of where segmentation, backup independence and identity privilege actually sit.

What to watch

Watch whether the refrigeration attacks in France turn out to be connected, because that would move this from an IT outage story to a story about physical operations and OT exposure in retail. Watch the Dutch regulator's account of the notification timeline. And watch the US brands, where a second disruption to pharmacy and delivery inside two years invites attention from state attorneys general as much as from customers.

The practical takeaway, for anyone running a business with a storefront and an app: write the shutdown decision down now. Who decides, on what signal, with what authority, and how the business keeps trading while the system is dark.
