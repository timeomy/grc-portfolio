ICANN Is Building a Signed Door for Police to Reach Hidden WHOIS Data

ICANN is testing SAML logins with the FBI and Interpol so vetted investigators can request non-public WHOIS data, with registrars holding a 24-hour clock.

Source: The Register

What happened

ICANN has started testing authentication schemes that would let law enforcement agencies file urgent requests for non-public domain registration data. The intended scope is narrow: threats to life, serious bodily injury, critical infrastructure, or child exploitation.

Domain registrars collect data about whoever registers a domain, which used to sit in public WHOIS records. Privacy services now substitute their own contact details, and laws such as the GDPR often require registrars to withhold the data. That protects registrants, and it also makes it harder for investigators to reach a domain holder when the domain hosts malware, runs command and control infrastructure, or carries a phishing page.

ICANN convened an Input Group on Law Enforcement Agency Authentication Mechanisms, which described its progress at the organisation's annual meeting in Bali. The preferred approach uses Security Assertion Markup Language so that authenticated users at an agency can log into ICANN's Registration Data Request Service and file a request. ICANN is already testing this with the FBI and Interpol, with the aim of publishing a specification other agencies can conform to.

ICANN passes a request to whichever registrar sold the domain. The registrar has 24 hours to consider it, or up to 72 hours for complex queries, and can refuse a request that does not meet the urgency threshold. To stop an agency flooding registrars with requests from thousands of agents, law enforcement bodies must nominate which personnel may file, and a token shared during the SAML handshake enforces that. Namecheap and Verisign both spoke in support. The content of that token is a policy question the technical group expects other parts of ICANN to settle later.

Why this is a GRC story

This is what a controlled exception looks like. Privacy law removed a default flow of information, and the response is not to reopen it but to build a narrow, authenticated, logged channel with a defined urgency test. The constraint is worth studying: an emergency route that anyone can use is not an emergency route, it is a bypass.

The urgency threshold is the whole control. "Imminent threat to life, of serious bodily injury, to critical infrastructure, or of child exploitation" is a policy statement, and it will be interpreted thousands of times a year by people in a hurry. Registrars can deny a request that does not qualify, which makes compliance staff at registrars the decision point for a law enforcement demand. That is an operational burden, and it belongs in a documented procedure with an escalation path.

Attribution and audit are the assurance. A token identifies which officer asked, for which domain, and when. That record is what makes the scheme defensible to a privacy regulator and to the public. If the audit trail is thin, the credibility of the mechanism goes with it.

What to watch

Watch the specification when it is published, particularly how agencies enrol, how tokens are issued and revoked, and whether registrars get a shared view of request patterns so one agency cannot spread volume across many of them.

For registrars and hosting providers, someone needs to own the 24-hour clock, know what evidence justifies a denial, and keep records that would satisfy a supervisory authority years later. That is a compliance process, not a technical one, and it is easier to write before the first urgent request arrives than during it.
