A GRU Crew Spent a Week Inside a NATO Shipbuilder Before Anyone Noticed

Symantec linked APT28 to intrusions at a naval shipbuilder, a military aircraft engine maker and a NATO law enforcement agency.

Source: CyberScoop

What happened

Symantec researchers published findings on an espionage campaign against three organisations in NATO countries: a naval shipbuilder, a manufacturer of military aircraft engines, and a law enforcement agency. The group is tracked as Swallowtail, better known as APT28, Fancy Bear or Forest Blizzard, and linked by Western governments to Russia's GRU.

The detail that stands out is dwell time. In the shipbuilder's network, Symantec observed hands-on-keyboard activity, with operators returning to a compromised host repeatedly across a week to collect credential material.

"Hands-on-keyboard activity means a human operator is choosing what to do next, rather than malware running a fixed routine," Dick O'Brien, principal intelligence analyst at the Symantec Threat Hunter Team, told CyberScoop. He described it as confirmation that the organisation was a genuine target rather than an accidental infection, and that further exploitation was at least attempted.

The operators used the Hookedge backdoor, first identified in August by Recorded Future's Insikt Group, which traced the same tool against defense and diplomatic targets in Romania, Spain and Turkey back to September of last year. Symantec also saw a previously undocumented loader, ld.exe, which decrypts its payload in memory and needs a key supplied only at runtime on the command line. The researchers did not recover the payload and do not know what it does.

O'Brien said the campaign reflects broader intelligence collection against European government and defense targets, though the motive is not certain. He suggested it could be tied to campaigns against nations supporting Ukraine.

Why this is a GRC story

A week of return visits is a detection failure, not a malware failure. The loader was unknown, but the activity was human and repeated. Someone opened sessions, gathered credentials and came back. That pattern lives in identity logs, remote access logs and process creation telemetry. If those logs are retained for ninety days everywhere except the jump hosts where it matters, the organisation has no chance of seeing it.

Espionage does not arrive with a ransom note. Most incident response playbooks are built around extortion: contain, assess, negotiate, restore. An intrusion that takes credential material and leaves quietly triggers none of those steps, and it may not trigger a breach notification if the data taken is not personal data. The governance question is which team owns an incident with no regulatory filing attached, because silence is often misread as reassurance.

Supplier obligations stop at the contract, not at the risk. CMMC and DFARS reach prime contractors. The sub tier suppliers holding the design data are often out of scope on paper and fully in scope in reality, and flow down is the control that decides whether the requirement means anything.

What to watch

Watch the government response, usually sanctions, a joint advisory, or the tooling added to a list vendors must detect. Hookedge detection content already exists. The unknown loader is the part that will take time.

For defenders in defense adjacent sectors, the useful work this week is unglamorous. Confirm that command line arguments are logged on endpoints and servers, that remote access sessions are retained long enough to cover a week of return visits, and that privilege reviews cover the service accounts an operator would target first. None of that catches an unknown loader, but all of it catches the human deploying one.
