Britain Wants 14 Years in Prison for Cutting a Subsea Cable

A UK white paper proposes 14 year sentences for intentional cable damage, unlimited fines for ship owners and new security duties on cable operators.

Source: Help Net Security

What happened

The UK government published a white paper this week that would rewrite the laws protecting undersea cables and pipelines, some of which date back 140 years. It comes from the Department for Digital, Culture, Media and Sport and opens a twelve week consultation.

Four changes carry the weight. Damage to undersea energy and telecoms infrastructure becomes an offence whether intentional, reckless or negligent, with up to 14 years in prison for intentional damage. Ship owners whose vessels cause damage face unlimited fines. Subsea telecoms cable operators take on security and resilience duties, including stronger requirements to identify and manage risk. And the Secretary of State gains a national security direction power over providers of cable infrastructure and repair capability.

A separate regime is proposed for fibre optic sensing technology on subsea cables. The government says the technology can detect activity at sea but could create national security risk if sensitive data fell into the wrong hands.

Undersea cables carry around 99% of the UK's international data, and undersea pipelines deliver 74% of its gas. Officials link the overhaul to suspicious Russian activity, after the Armed Forces exposed a covert Russian submarine operation around undersea infrastructure in April. They also stress that most faults come from anchors, trawler nets, environmental hazards or technical failure, not sabotage.

Why this is a GRC story

Intent becomes a documented question. The penalties are graded by state of mind. Intentional, reckless and negligent are three evidentiary standards, and for a ship owner the line between reckless and negligent will be drawn by records: passage plans, watchkeeping, notices to mariners and what the bridge crew knew. Where a duty is written that way, the compliance artefact is also the defence.

The security duties put cable operators where telecoms providers have sat for years, with an obligation to identify and manage risk and a direction power behind it. A risk register entry is no longer the end of the work, because a direction can require specific measures and the operator has to show it can deliver them.

Repair capability being named in the direction power is the part to notice. Mean time to repair is usually an availability metric owned by engineering. Treating it as a security control means it needs an owner, evidence and a test, the way a recovery objective does. The sensing regime is a small lesson in dual use governance, since the same fibre that detects a dragging anchor can detect a submarine.

What to watch

The consultation will decide whether the security duties arrive with a code of practice and reporting obligations or as broad principles. Watch how far the direction power reaches, whether it covers subcontractors and landing stations, and how insurance responds to unlimited fines. Watch whether the sensing rules set a precedent that pulls port monitoring and pipeline integrity into scope.

For anyone with infrastructure exposure, the first step is small. Map which obligations already depend on a physical route you do not control, and who you would call if that route were severed for three weeks.
