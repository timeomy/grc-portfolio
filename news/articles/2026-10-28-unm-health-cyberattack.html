New Mexico's Only Level 1 Trauma Center Diverted Ambulances After a Cyberattack

A cyberattack on UNM's Health Sciences Center forced ambulance diversions and cancelled clinics, while an unknown group claimed staff and student data.

Source: Help Net Security

What happened

The University of New Mexico Health Sciences Center, the academic medical arm of the state's public research university, disclosed a cybersecurity event on Monday afternoon. The clinical side includes University of New Mexico Hospital, the state's largest teaching hospital, and the health system operates New Mexico's only Level 1 trauma center.

The operational impact arrived before any explanation. At 2:30 pm local time the center announced it was diverting ambulance traffic to other hospitals and cancelling all appointments at all clinics for the day. Later updates said IT security teams were containing the event, external partners had been engaged, state and federal law enforcement were notified, and systems were being brought back online in phases. Leadership activated an Emergency Operations Center.

By the evening, all UNM Health hospitals were open and clinics were due to resume appointments and procedures on Tuesday. Classes at the medicine, nursing, pharmacy and other schools were cancelled, and by Tuesday that had stretched to a second day per The Register, with nonessential staff asked to stay home. The center said it was limited in what it could share because the law enforcement investigation remained active.

No cause has been confirmed. According to The Daily Lobo, the student newspaper, hackers played a synthetic voice message on hospital workers' computers claiming they had taken student and employee data, personal identifiable information, Social Security numbers, private documents and email, and inviting administrators to make contact through an encrypted messaging service. The Register reported that a newly assembled group called BYOD claimed responsibility on Monday, asserting it had compromised 36,000 machines, both Active Directory forests, two Oracle databases and personal data for every student and employee. Those claims are unverified, and a group taking credit does not establish involvement.

Why this is a GRC story

Clinical downtime is the risk statement. Diversion at the only Level 1 trauma center in a state is patient harm in the making, and it happened before anyone knew the cause. Resilience planning that stops at recovery time objectives misses this: the control that mattered on Monday was a written diversion plan and phone trees that worked at 2:30 pm.

Containment and communication collide. The centre was simultaneously running an active investigation, a law enforcement request and a public statement, and chose to say little. That is defensible, but the clock on patient and workforce notification keeps running regardless, and the messaging has to be drafted before the incident, not during it.

Extortion without proof is still an event. The threat actors skipped the usual leak site choreography and went straight at staff, which pressures the organization to respond publicly to claims it may not be able to confirm for weeks. A named incident lead, a pre-approved holding statement and a documented decision log are what keep that pressure from shaping the facts.

What to watch

Whether UNM confirms the scope, particularly the claim touching both Active Directory forests and student records, since that would move this from availability incident toward a reportable breach with student financial aid data implications. Watch whether the group reuses the voice message tactic on other healthcare victims, and whether regulators open an inquiry given the interruption to trauma care. The sector's response time to the next one will say more than any after action report.
