FTC and 20 States Sue Dollar General Over Register Prices

The FTC and 20 states allege Dollar General knew about tens of thousands of overcharging complaints and failed to fix its pricing controls.

Source: Federal Trade Commission

What happened

The Federal Trade Commission, joined by 20 states, filed a complaint against Dollar General on Monday in the U.S. District Court for the Middle District of Tennessee. It alleges long running deceptive pricing: customers were routinely charged more at the register than the prices on shelves, price tags and advertisements, costing millions of dollars.

Dollar General operates more than 21,000 stores nationwide, the vast majority in small towns, and markets itself as a low priced alternative for households on tight budgets. The FTC's consumer protection chief, Christopher Mufarrige, said the company had betrayed the trust of millions of consumers and imposed financial harm on communities already struggling to make paychecks last.

The complaint's central allegation is not that pricing errors happened. It is that they kept happening after the company knew. Thousands of stores used insufficient safeguards to prevent overcharging, and Dollar General received tens of thousands of complaints from consumers saying it did not honour advertised prices. The FTC alleges the company understood it was harming consumers, as reflected in that complaint volume, and still failed to implement adequate policies and practices.

The FTC and the states allege violations of the FTC Act, the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act and state laws. The states joining are Alabama, Arizona, Arkansas, California, Connecticut, Florida, Illinois, Iowa, Kansas, Louisiana, Michigan, Mississippi, Nebraska, New Jersey, North Carolina, Ohio, Oklahoma, Tennessee, Utah and Wisconsin. The Commission vote authorising the filing was 2-0. A complaint reflects the agency's belief that conduct is unlawful; the court decides.

Why this is a GRC story

Complaint volume is a risk signal with a timestamp. Every complaint in that stack was a free early warning. The regulator's theory is knowledge plus inaction, which makes the complaint log the evidence. If you measure complaints for resolution speed and not for control failure, you collect the data with none of the benefit.

Price accuracy is a control, and it needs an owner. Shelf prices, the price file and the point of sale system are three sources of truth that drift apart silently. Reconciling them is unglamorous work, usually owned by nobody in particular and sampled too rarely to spot a systematic gap.

Vulnerable customers aggravate harm. The FTC leans on the fact that these stores serve low and fixed income households who rely on price accuracy. Harm to people who cannot absorb it turns a technical violation into a priority case.

The statutory mix is unusual. Alongside the FTC Act and state law, the complaint cites the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, hardly the obvious vehicle for a shelf price dispute. When a regulator reaches for a second statute, it is usually trying to reach something the first one cannot.

What to watch

Whether discovery puts internal complaint dashboards and store level audit results into the public record, and how the court treats a company that had the data and did not act. Also whether the multistate coalition encourages state attorneys general to examine other discount chains. For retail compliance teams the durable lesson is cheaper than the litigation: a pricing control nobody samples is not a control.
