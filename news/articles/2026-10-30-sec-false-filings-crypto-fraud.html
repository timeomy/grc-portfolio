SEC Says False Filings Were the Sales Material in a $130 Million Scheme

The SEC charged two Colorado-incorporated entities with using false Form ADV and Form D filings to make a $130 million crypto scheme look legitimate.

Source: SEC Press Releases

What happened

The Securities and Exchange Commission charged two foreign operated, Colorado incorporated entities, BG Wealth Sharing Ltd and DSJ Exchange Pty Ltd, over a scheme that raised more than $130 million from tens of thousands of retail investors. The complaint was filed in the Northern District of California and covers conduct from at least October 2025 through June 2026.

According to the SEC, the pair sold a supposed wealth sharing programme with promises of extraordinary, risk free returns generated by trading signals for crypto asset trading. BG Wealth claimed to be the world's largest hedge fund, registered with the SEC as an investment adviser, and partnered with a licensed and secure crypto platform. The regulator says none of that was true, and that the defendants created the appearance of legitimacy by submitting false Form ADV and Form D filings. Those filings are publicly viewable, and the complaint says they were shown to investors as proof that the SEC had approved the business.

The outreach was deliberate: social media, encrypted messaging, weekly live videoconferences and a mobile app. The complaint alleges the defendants displayed fake certificates bearing the SEC seal and made periodic payments to some investors, a Ponzi like tactic, while misappropriating the rest. Laura D'Allaird, who leads the SEC's Cyber and Emerging Technologies Unit, said the scheme was well planned and built to keep investors engaged.

The complaint charges both entities under the antifraud provisions of the Securities Act of 1933 and the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, and charges BG Wealth under sections 204(a) and 207 of the Investment Advisers Act of 1940. The SEC seeks injunctions, disgorgement with prejudgment interest and civil penalties. Its investor education office has warned that scammers use exempt reporting adviser filings to imply SEC approval.

Why this is a GRC story

A filing is a notice, not a licence. Form ADV and Form D record that a submission was made. Nothing about their existence speaks to whether the business is real, and the scheme worked because investors treated a public record as a regulatory stamp.

Marketing approval is a compliance control. Claims about registration, assets under management and regulated partnerships all passed through someone's review before reaching investors. Where that review does not demand evidence for each specific claim, marketing becomes an uncontrolled channel for misrepresentation.

The partner claim is a third party risk question. One entity was presented as a licensed and secure platform. Repeat a partner's licensing claims in your own materials and you have adopted them as your own. Verification has to reach the regulator's register, not a screenshot.

Payments as evidence of legitimacy. Early payouts are the oldest trust mechanic in fraud and they work on sophisticated people too. The lesson is procedural: verification has to happen before funds move, not after the first payment arrives.

What to watch

Whether the court freezes assets or appoints a receiver, since recovery usually turns on the first filing. The check any compliance team can run in minutes is to read an adviser's Form ADV on the SEC register rather than counting filings, and to require the same of any partner whose licence you intend to repeat.
