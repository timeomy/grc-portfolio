SEC Carves Out Inter-Affiliate and Non-U.S. Repo From Treasury Clearing

The SEC granted targeted exemptive relief from Treasury clearing rules for certain inter-affiliate and non-U.S. repo transactions, with limits attached.

Source: U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission

What happened

The Securities and Exchange Commission issued exemptive relief on 30 October from parts of the Treasury clearing rules it adopted in December 2023. The relief narrows what counts as an eligible secondary market transaction in two specific areas rather than changing the rules themselves.

The first is certain repo transactions involving non-U.S. counterparties, subject to specified limits and conditions. The second is certain repo transactions between a direct participant of a U.S. Treasury securities clearing agency and any affiliated entity that is not an investment company as defined in Section 3 of the Investment Company Act of 1940, provided that affiliate otherwise meets the definition of an affiliated counterparty in Rule 17ad-22(a).

The relief follows requests the SEC published for comment on behalf of market participants, and it continues a sequence of accommodations. The Commission extended the original compliance dates and provided a temporary exemption earlier, then launched a single implementation page in September 2025 that consolidates Commission and staff statements, FAQs and other resources. Commissioner Mark T. Uyeda, whom Chairman Paul S. Atkins designated to lead the transition to central clearing, described the relief as targeted. He said successful implementation has to account for the realities of cross-border transactions, and that the relief addresses practical concerns while preserving the benefits of central clearing. The order is published as Release 34-106789.

Why this is a GRC story

Relief is a scoping change, and scoping changes are where control gaps appear. Every entity that now sits outside the eligible secondary market transaction definition still has to show it meets the conditions, and those conditions are the kind that get tested months later by someone reading a file. Reading the order and mapping each condition to a control, with a named owner and evidence of operation, is a week of work that prevents a much worse conversation.

Cross-border means two rulebooks at once. Non-U.S. counterparties bring their own clearing, netting and collateral conventions. Anyone who read the relief as permission to relax should notice the phrase subject to specified limits and conditions. Limits are monitored, which means reporting, a defined measurement basis and a break process for when a threshold is approached rather than crossed.

Affiliate relief is not blanket. The carve-out reaches affiliated counterparties that are not investment companies under the 1940 Act. An affiliate that is a fund stays in scope. The entity that wears two hats, direct participant on one trade and affiliate on the next, is exactly where booking and eligibility errors happen, so the control has to live at the trade level, not the entity level.

What to watch

The compliance dates have already moved once, so the question is whether the next accommodation arrives as staff guidance or as another exemption. The two require different governance responses: guidance can be adopted operationally, an exemption has to be evidenced and tracked. The Division of Trading and Markets is taking questions directly by email and phone, and answers given through that channel tend to become the practical standard long before anything formal is published. Firms should be logging those exchanges as interpretive decisions rather than letting them live in an inbox.
