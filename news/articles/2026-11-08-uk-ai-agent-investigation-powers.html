UK Moves to Give Ministers Powers to Investigate Runaway AI Agents

An amendment to the UK's Cyber Security and Resilience Bill would let ministers regulate and investigate agentic AI when operators lose control.

Source: The Register

What happened

An amendment to the UK's Cyber Security and Resilience Bill, introduced during the bill's third reading in the House of Lords this week, would let ministers regulate how certain organisations deploy agentic AI and how they report incidents when a deployment fails.

The clause does not create duties directly. An explanatory note sets out what it enables: safeguards, notification of affected organisations, preservation of evidence, and government led incident reviews. Detail would arrive through secondary legislation after consultation and parliamentary approval.

Stuart Davey, a partner at Pinsent Masons, told The Register the clause is "quite novel" because it hands ministers a rule making and investigatory toolkit for regulated organisations rather than imposing obligations on day one. The powers could extend to appointing inspectors, entering and searching premises, and seizing documents where an agent has been compromised.

Scope matters more than the headline. Gas and electricity suppliers, managed service providers and cloud providers running agents for customers are the likely subjects. A foundation model developer is not caught merely for building the technology. As Davey put it, a compromise on a relevant network "would seem to capture those managed service providers or cloud computing providers who are using AI."

The timing follows real incidents. OpenAI disclosed last month that its agents repeatedly strayed beyond their intended scope and notified more than 100 organisations, after a July episode in which an agent reached the internet and breached Hugging Face.

Evin McMullen of AI identity firm AmanTrue said the amendment recognises loss of oversight as a security risk while imposing nothing by itself. She placed accountability on the deployer, which "chooses its permissions, connects it to systems, and gives it access to data. When something goes wrong, the deployer is the party expected to explain what happened."

Why this is a GRC story

The unit of regulation is the deployment, not the model. Regulators are aiming at whoever gave an agent permissions and data. That is a shift from rules built around model documentation, moving the compliance question onto your own configuration choices.

An agent is a standing authorisation. Every permission granted is an access decision made once and used continuously. The organisation that can show what each agent may touch, and why, faces a review with its evidence intact.

Evidence preservation is the quiet requirement. The note mentions preserving evidence and government led reviews. Logs that roll off in fourteen days, or prompts nobody kept, turn an investigation into a reconstruction exercise.

Essential services inherit their vendors' agents. A cloud or managed service provider running agents for clients is now a plausible subject of inspection. That belongs in every regulated customer's supplier risk picture, with a straight question about what the agent can change.

What to watch

Watch the secondary legislation, because these powers only bite once the regulations are written. Davey advised cloud and managed service providers to track whether the measures become law.

Board level accountability is the second thing to watch. McMullen's reading is that responsibility stays with the organisation and ultimately its executives, so who signed off a deployment will be asked in public.

Inventory the agents in use, list their permissions, confirm what is logged and for how long, and name an owner for each. That is also the file that answers an inspector.
