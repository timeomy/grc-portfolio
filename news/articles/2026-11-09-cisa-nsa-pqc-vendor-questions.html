CISA, NSA and Australia's ACSC Turn Post Quantum Risk Into Procurement Questions

Joint guidance gives procurement and vendor teams a structured set of questions to test whether suppliers can deliver a post quantum transition.

Source: CISA

What happened

On November 5, the Australian Signals Directorate's Australian Cyber Security Centre, CISA and the National Security Agency published joint guidance called Post quantum questions to ask your vendors. It sets out vendor neutral questions for procurement, contract renewals and ongoing vendor assurance, plus a quick reference list. The Australian centre wrote the original in July. The November release is a three agency version with more background.

The premise is simple. Most organisations do not implement their own cryptography. They inherit it from cloud providers, managed services, appliances and software, and those vendors decide which algorithms run and when they change. The guidance says plainly that an organisation's transition will depend on its vendors' readiness, transparency and roadmaps. A strong internal migration plan does not survive a supplier that never ships.

The questions are grouped by transition phase: locate and inventory cryptographic dependencies, assess risk to individual systems, triage and prioritise systems for transition, implement post quantum algorithms, and communicate with vendors while educating stakeholders. A closing section covers common concerns in vendor responses and how to handle them.

Several questions are awkward for suppliers in useful ways. Does the vendor maintain a cryptographic bill of materials? Is any cryptography hardcoded or bound to hardware that cannot be updated? Does the product support hybrid modes during the migration? How will larger keys and certificates affect performance and lifecycle management? Is post quantum support in the base licence or sold as an upgrade?

The guidance is advisory and creates no obligations. What makes it worth reading is who it names as the audience: security leaders, procurement teams, vendor management and technical stakeholders. The questions only work if the person holding the renewal asks them.

Why this is a GRC story

You cannot migrate what you have not inventoried. The first phase is an asset register problem, and it is usually the phase that fails. Cryptographic dependencies hide in libraries, firmware, certificates and equipment with fifteen year lifecycles, and most of them were documented by nobody.

The vendor answer is an assurance finding. A supplier that cannot produce a cryptographic inventory has told you something auditable. That answer belongs in the risk register next to the dependency, with an owner and a date. An assessment with no evidence and no owner is a conversation, not a control.

Licensing is a governance decision. If post quantum support arrives as a paid upgrade, the cost lands on the customer at the moment of maximum urgency. Knowing that now changes budget planning, and it gives renewal negotiations something concrete to trade.

Long lived equipment is the hard edge. Controllers and sensors that cannot be updated are not an IT problem. They are a physical risk with a replacement cost, and someone has to decide whether to replace, isolate or formally accept them.

What to watch

Watch whether sector regulators fold these questions into supervisory expectations. Guidance issued by three national agencies tends to become the reference text that examiners quote, and procurement templates follow the examiners.

The useful step this quarter is to pick the ten suppliers that touch your most sensitive data, ask two questions from the list, and write down what comes back. The answers will tell you more about your real migration timeline than any internal workshop.
