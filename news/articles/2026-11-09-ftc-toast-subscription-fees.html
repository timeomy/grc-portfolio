FTC Sues Toast Over $0 Pricing, Hidden Fees and a Cancellation Maze

The FTC and eight states say Toast buried fees, billed add ons without consent and made cancelling a subscription a multi step ordeal.

Source: Federal Trade Commission

What happened

On October 29, the Federal Trade Commission, the people of California and the attorneys general of seven states sued Toast, a Boston company selling point of sale software, payments and payroll tools to restaurants. The complaint, filed in the Northern District of California, names Toast and five affiliates including lending arm Toast Capital.

The core allegation is that the advertised price was not the price paid. Toast marketed a pay as you go plan with hardware and software at $0 a month, and the complaint says the fees tied to it sat in fine print or late in checkout. Customers who did not begin processing payments within 120 days had to return the hardware or pay its retail value. Restaurants that closed temporarily owed $85 monthly. Cancelling early carried a fee of $150 for every remaining month, a term the FTC says sat in the middle of a 25 page merchant agreement.

The complaint also alleges Toast used stored bank details to bill renewals and add on subscriptions without affirmative consent, and kept billing after they asked it to stop. Between September 2021 and July 2023 it added a 99 cent fee to some orders over $10, kept the money rather than passing it to the restaurant, and buried it in a taxes and fees line.

Sign up took an online form. Cancelling required written notice 30 days before renewal, a call to customer care where staff pitched retention, transfer to a non public line open weekdays only, a deactivation form during a call of roughly 15 minutes, and back office processing. Borrowers from Toast Capital were told that switching provider counted as an event of default, making the loan due. One customer was charged $5,318.87 to cancel after four years and refused a refund.

The FTC pleads violations of Section 5 of the FTC Act, the Restore Online Shoppers' Confidence Act and state law. Toast says it strongly disagrees and will defend the case, arguing the statute was written for consumer subscriptions and does not reach negotiated business agreements.

Why this is a GRC story

Disclosure is a control, not a paragraph. The complaint does not allege Toast lacked terms. It alleges they sat where nobody would read them. A control that depends on a hyperlink in the middle of an agreement is not operating.

Support promises are service commitments. Advertising round the clock support while staffing a cancellation line on weekday hours is a gap between what the contract sells and what operations delivers. The complaint also leaned on internal surveys, chat logs and complaint records. A complaints queue nobody reads is a liability with a timestamp.

Billing needs a consent record. Every charge after the first rests on an affirmative decision. If the only evidence is a stored bank account, the organisation cannot show when consent was given or what it covered.

What to watch

The legal question is whether the statute reaches business to business sales. The Eighth Circuit vacated the FTC's click to cancel rule in 2025, so these cases rest on Section 5, tested case by case, while states legislate in the gap.

The practical step is to walk your cancellation path with a stopwatch, then check that every fee a customer can incur appears before they enter payment details.
