AI Adoption Is Straining GDPR Programmes, Data Experts Warn

Reporting says AI tools' appetite for data sits awkwardly against GDPR duties on minimisation, consent and telling people what their data is used for.

Source: Compliance Week

What happened

Compliance Week reported on November 5 that data experts are warning of a collision between the way companies now use artificial intelligence and the rules they spent the last decade building programmes around. The argument is short. AI tools need more data to function and to improve, while the General Data Protection Regulation asks organisations to collect as little as they can justify. The two pressures do not reconcile by themselves.

The reported concern goes beyond volume. It is that data is being used without consent, and without telling people what is held and why. A company can hold a complete record of processing, a signed off privacy notice and a trained workforce, and still find that a tool adopted by one team last quarter pulls personal data into a purpose nobody documented.

That is uncomfortable for anyone who treated GDPR compliance as a project that finished. The reporting suggests the finish line moved, not because the law changed, but because the processing did.

Why this is a GRC story

The principles did not change. The processing did. Data minimisation, purpose limitation and transparency are the same tests they were in 2018. What changed is that a single AI feature can widen collection, add a new purpose and take a person out of the loop, all inside a product update that nobody reviewed.

Paperwork that was true last year is now wrong. A record of processing that lists a supplier but not the model embedded in that supplier's product is inaccurate. An accountability argument resting on documents that no longer describe the system is hard to defend and harder to evidence. Article 5 of the regulation puts the burden on the organisation to demonstrate compliance, not merely to assert it.

The control that usually fails is purpose, not security. Most programmes can show encryption, access control and logging. Fewer can show that a dataset is still used only for the purpose it was collected for once a language model has been pointed at it. That is where an audit conversation gets quiet.

Consent is a design decision, not a banner. If a tool improves by learning from the data it receives, the moment of collection has to carry that information, and the notice has to describe it in words a person can act on. A general clause about service improvement does not do that work.

Vendors move the boundary without asking. When a supplier adds an AI feature to software you already bought, your processing changes and your assessment does not. The gap between those two events is the risk, and it is a third party risk question as much as a privacy one.

What to watch

Watch the regulators rather than the vendors. Guidance and enforcement decisions from European authorities and the UK regulator are the practical boundary, and they will land before any legislative tidy up. Watch also whether privacy impact assessments get refreshed when a supplier ships AI features, because that refresh is the cheapest place to catch the gap.

The question worth asking this week is unglamorous. Which tools in your estate now send personal data to a model, who approved that, and what purpose does the record of processing actually name?
