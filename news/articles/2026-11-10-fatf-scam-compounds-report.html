FATF Tells Members to Attack Scam Compounds Upstream, Not Just Trace the Money

FATF says scam compounds drive a $500 billion fraud economy and wants members disrupting recruitment, domains and payment rails before victims pay.

Source: Financial Action Task Force

What happened

The Financial Action Task Force has published a report on illicit financial flows linked to scam compounds, and it is blunt about scale. FATF describes large physical compounds as a major driver of a global fraud economy it estimates at $500 billion a year. In Myanmar alone, it says 120,000 trafficked people are forced to carry out online scams.

The operations are not self contained. FATF points to crypto and instant payment platforms, money mules, social media, messaging services and AI enabled content as the supporting infrastructure that lets stolen money move and lets new victims get found. It also describes a shift toward smaller and more mobile setups: repurposed casinos, rented rooms, apartments, coffee shops, and in some cases moving vehicles.

The central finding is about where enforcement effort currently sits. FATF says countries are generally better at downstream action, meaning asset tracing and going after victim payments once money has already left. It wants members to pair that with upstream disruption aimed at fraudulent advertising, recruitment activity, malicious domains, and suspicious phone numbers and telecom indicators.

The report sets out six pillars: shift focus from physical compounds to the criminal leaders, recruiters and traffickers behind them; disrupt the infrastructure and services, including stopping, tracing, freezing and recovering victim payments; share information between financial institutions, technology companies, telcos and authorities; act at speed across borders, since the first 24 to 72 hours are critical for freezing funds; put protection of trafficked and scam victims at the centre of investigations; and keep updating the approach as operations decentralise.

FATF president Giles Thomson framed it as a modernisation problem, saying criminals are weaponising AI and other technologies to intensify scam operations and that the response needs to accelerate. Jonathan Frost, director of global advisory at BioCatch, welcomed the focus on compounds and on action by platform providers.

Why this is a GRC story

AML programmes are trained on the wrong end of the pipe. Most financial crime controls are built to spot suspicious movement after the fact. FATF is arguing that the cheaper intervention sits earlier, in the advertising, recruitment and infrastructure layer that a bank never sees but a platform does.

Information sharing is a governance decision. One of the six pillars asks institutions to share across sectors that do not normally talk. Doing that requires a lawful basis, a defined data set, and someone accountable for what leaves the building. That is a policy question before it is a technical one.

The victim is now a control objective. Putting trafficked workers at the centre of investigations changes what an investigation is for. It also creates a duty of care question for any institution that discovers them during a review.

What to watch

Watch whether FATF members publish guidance that reaches advertising platforms and telcos rather than just banks, since that is where the report puts the leverage. For compliance teams at payment firms, the immediate test is whether a suspected scam report triggers a freezing action inside the 24 to 72 hour window, or whether it waits in a queue and misses it.
