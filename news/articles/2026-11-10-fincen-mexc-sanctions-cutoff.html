FinCEN Cuts MEXC Global Off From the US Financial System Over $16 Billion in Illicit Flows

FinCEN says MEXC Global moved at least $16 billion tied to sanctions evasion and cybercrime, and US banks now have 30 days to stop touching it.

Source: US Department of the Treasury

What happened

On November 9, the US Department of the Treasury announced that its Financial Crimes Enforcement Network had issued an order effectively cutting MEXC Global off from the US financial system. MEXC is a convertible virtual currency exchanger, founded in 2018, that processed more than $1 billion in daily transactions as of September 2026 and held roughly $5 billion in reserves. It sits among the twenty largest exchanges in the world and offers trading in more than 3,000 crypto assets.

The order rests on section 9714(a) of the Combating Russian Money Laundering Act. It bars US financial institutions from any transmittal of funds from or to MEXC, and it takes effect 30 days after publication in the Federal Register. The action was announced as part of something Treasury calls Operation Economic Outcast.

The numbers in the order are the story. Between May 2021 and May 2026, FinCEN says MEXC moved at least $16 billion connected to sanctions evasion, cybercrime and other illicit activity. That includes nearly $1.5 billion linked to Russian sanctions evasion, with $232 million in direct transactions with entities already sanctioned by the US, the EU and the UK. Treasury says MEXC was still processing transactions with Garantex and its successor Grinex as recently as June 2026, more than four years after Garantex was designated in April 2022.

The order also cites roughly $1 billion in direct transactions with more than 40 identified Iranian exchangers, about $22 million involving the Central Bank of Iran and the IRGC and its Qods Force, and almost $200 million connected to the A7A5 stablecoin used by a sanctioned evasion network. Treasury attributes all of it to severe deficiencies in MEXC's anti-money laundering and customer identification controls.

MEXC said its legal and compliance teams are reviewing the order, that the platform is operating normally, and that it has begun an independent review of its AML and sanctions controls.

Why this is a GRC story

A policy is not a control. MEXC reportedly prohibited use by people in Iran and North Korea. The order says that prohibition did not stop the transactions. The gap between what a policy document states and what a screening system actually blocks is where enforcement lands, and it is the same gap examiners look for in any institution.

Sanctions are a maintenance obligation. The Garantex and Grinex point is the sharpest one. A designation from April 2022 should have been reflected in counterparty screening long before June 2026. Sanctions lists change constantly, and a program that was tested once at onboarding is not a program.

Secondary exposure is real. US institutions and individuals now face enforcement risk for moving funds to or from MEXC. Treasury notes that the order does not modify existing Bank Secrecy Act duties, including suspicious activity reporting, or OFAC obligations. Compliance teams inherit work rather than relief.

What to watch

Watch how firms with any crypto or remittance exposure evidence their counterparty review between now and the effective date. Watch too whether other jurisdictions follow, since a US cut off often precedes action elsewhere. For defenders, the practical step is boring and unglamorous: pull the vendor and counterparty list, check it against current designations, and write down who checked and when.
