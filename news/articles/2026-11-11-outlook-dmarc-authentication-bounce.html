Microsoft's Spam Crackdown Bounced Legitimate Email, and Small Senders Paid

Outlook.com rejected valid mail for days after a Microsoft rule update, catching domains with missing DMARC records or failing SPF checks.

Source: The Register

What happened

Outlook.com stopped accepting legitimate email for several days, according to users who reported non-delivery messages carrying SPF validation errors, with others seeing errors about missing DMARC information. The Register reported the trouble began at the end of last week and hit Microsoft's consumer email services.

Microsoft had announced rules in 2025 aimed at domains sending more than 5,000 emails a day, requiring DMARC and SPF compliance plus DKIM. What appears to have happened in November is that tightened enforcement caught far smaller senders. Users reported that a single line message could be rejected. The three technical conditions in play are simple to state: SPF has to pass for the sending domain so its DNS record lists the authorised hosts, DKIM has to pass to prove integrity, and DMARC has to exist with at least p=none and align with SPF or DKIM.

The failure was also inconsistent. A resend could succeed after a rejection, and the bounce messages were vague about the cause. Microsoft did not publish a customer advisory explaining what had changed, and some affected administrators insisted their records were already compliant while others found that correcting SPF, DKIM, and DMARC restored delivery.

On November 10, Microsoft confirmed the fault. In incident report EX1545193, the company wrote that "a recent update to the spam enforcement rules caused email messages to be incorrectly marked and blocked, which led to impact." It said it would review its update procedures. A forum moderator had earlier described the problem as a service degradation while engineering teams analysed telemetry and customer data.

Why this is a GRC story

Authentication records are a control, not a checkbox. Most organisations treat SPF, DKIM, and DMARC as a one time DNS task. A domain that publishes DMARC at p=none is asking for reports, not enforcement, and it is the same domain that gets treated as suspicious the moment a large provider tightens its filters.

Third party filtering is an unmanaged dependency. No amount of internal control stops a receiving platform from rejecting valid mail. What a firm can control is whether it notices. Deliverability monitoring, DMARC aggregate reports, and a named owner for the sending domain turn an outage into a ticket instead of a silent loss of customer contact.

Change communication is part of the risk. Microsoft changed enforcement before it explained it, then confirmed a fault after users had already spent days troubleshooting their own infrastructure. Any organisation that sends into a large consumer mail provider inherits that exposure, and it belongs in the vendor and resilience conversation rather than only in the email team.

What to watch

Watch whether Microsoft publishes details of the review it promised of its update procedures, since that determines how often this recurs. For defenders, the practical step is to check three things this week: that DMARC exists and is aligned, that SPF stays under the lookup limit after every vendor change, and that someone is reading the aggregate reports.
