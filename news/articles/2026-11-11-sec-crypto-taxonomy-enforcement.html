The SEC's Crypto Taxonomy Is Also a Playbook for Open Enforcement Cases

A joint SEC and CFTC release reclassifies most digital assets and gives targets of pending crypto cases arguments that did not exist a year ago.

Source: Compliance Week

What happened

In a Compliance Week opinion piece published on November 10, Buchalter partner Ashwin J. Ram walks through a 68 page joint interpretive release that the SEC and the CFTC published on March 17, 2026, telling the industry what is and is not a security.

The release sorts digital assets into five categories. Digital commodities, digital collectibles, digital tools, and stablecoins as defined under the GENIUS Act are not securities. Digital securities, meaning tokenised versions of stocks, bonds, and fund interests, remain securities and the token wrapper changes nothing. That taxonomy alone would be the most significant crypto guidance the SEC has issued.

The novel part has no precedent in the federal securities laws: an investment contract that ceases to exist. Under Howey, a token sale can be an investment contract because buyers expect profits from the issuer's efforts. The release accepts that the same asset can later separate from that contract once the network is decentralised, the protocol works, and value no longer depends on the issuer's managerial efforts. Ram calls this a transformation, and notes that securities law has never recognised a security that stops being one as the underlying business matures.

That lands against a changed enforcement posture. Chairman Paul Atkins, speaking at SEC Speaks 2026, called the previous administration's regulation by enforcement a failure, and Commissioner Mark Uyeda described exchanges turned away when they tried to register. The article notes a harmonisation memorandum of understanding with the CFTC, work on an innovation exemption for tokenised securities platforms, and the dismissal or abandonment of more than a dozen crypto matters, including actions against Coinbase, Binance, Kraken, and Gemini, plus investigations into Uniswap Labs, OpenSea, Crypto.com, Robinhood, and Ondo Finance. In SEC v. Ripple Labs, the agency dropped its appeal and cross appeal.

Ram's advice to anyone still inside the machinery is concrete. A Wells submission should lead with the release and integrate the taxonomy and transformation reasoning. Firms in active litigation should test whether a motion to dismiss or a request for voluntary dismissal now fits. Firms that operated in the grey zone should map their token against the five categories, document the analysis, and preserve it.

Why this is a GRC story

Classification is a documented decision. The compliance work is not deciding that a token is a digital tool. It is recording who made that call, on what date, against which version of the release, and keeping the record retrievable years later. The piece says as much almost in passing, and it is the sentence that matters most to a compliance function.

Regulatory risk moved, it did not disappear. A narrower securities definition does not remove AML, sanctions, custody, marketing, or consumer protection obligations, and it does not settle how the categories are applied to any specific product.

Pendulum risk is a governance test. The same release that helps respondents today reflects a commission view that could change with its membership. Programs built to survive a reversal are the ones with written rationale rather than verbal assumptions.

What to watch

Watch how the SEC applies the transformation idea in the cases that remain live. Watch the innovation exemption for tokenised securities. And watch whether the five categories get tested in a contested proceeding, because an interpretive release is not a court ruling.
