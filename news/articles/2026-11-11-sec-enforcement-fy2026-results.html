SEC Closes Fiscal 2026 With 348 Enforcement Actions and $1.095 Billion in Relief

The SEC filed 348 enforcement actions in fiscal 2026, obtained $1.095 billion in monetary relief, and charged individuals in two thirds of standalone cases.

Source: US Securities and Exchange Commission

What happened

On November 10, the Securities and Exchange Commission published its enforcement results for fiscal year 2026, which ended September 30. The Division of Enforcement filed 348 actions: 235 standalone cases and 113 follow-on administrative proceedings that sought to bar or suspend people based on criminal convictions, civil injunctions, or other qualifying orders. The Commission also instituted 13 trading suspensions tied to suspected manipulation in the securities of Asia-based issuers.

Monetary relief reached $1.095 billion, made up of $752 million in disgorgement and prejudgment interest and $343 million in civil penalties. About $235 million of that disgorgement was deemed satisfied in a separate, non-SEC action. The agency says it returned roughly $731 million to more than 250,000 harmed investors. Two thirds of the standalone actions included charges against at least one individual.

Chairman Paul Atkins pushed back gently on reading the release as a scoreboard. Enforcement actions and financial remedies, he said, are "important measures of activity, but they are not the only or most telling measures."

The structural changes matter as much as the totals. The Division formed a Retail Fraud Working Group and a Financial Reporting and Accounting Unit, and it stood up an Office of Super Intelligence and Analytics that brings anomaly detection, accelerated document and transcript review, and blockchain and financial flow analysis into live investigations. It also updated the Enforcement Manual for the first time since 2017, standardising the Wells process and allowing simultaneous consideration of settlements and related waivers, and rescinded Rule 202.5(e), the policy that stopped the agency settling cases where the respondent refused to agree not to publicly deny the allegations.

Buried in the case list is the part to read twice. The Commission brought settled actions over Regulation S-P safeguards and identity theft red flags failures at M Holdings Securities, over Regulation SCI at the New York Stock Exchange after it failed to run opening auctions for more than 2,800 securities, over suspicious activity report filing failures at Merrill Lynch and Canaccord Genuity, over separation agreements at Foot Locker that appeared to waive whistleblower award rights under Rule 21F-17(a), and over incomplete blue sheet data at Wedbush.

Why this is a GRC story

The guardrail cases are the curriculum. Safeguards and red flags programs, SAR filing, SCI obligations, whistleblower carve outs, and trading data accuracy rarely make headlines. They are also exactly the controls a compliance team can test this quarter without a lawyer in the room.

Supervision is going analytical. The new analytics office is a signal about how cases will be found. Anomaly detection and financial flow analysis look for the pattern a firm's own monitoring was supposed to catch, and regulators get to compare notes with each other through memoranda of understanding with the CFTC, the FDA, and the PCAOB.

Process fairness cuts both ways. A standardised Wells process and the end of the no-deny settlement policy give respondents more room to argue. That raise the value of a clean, contemporaneous record built before anyone asks for it.

What to watch

Watch whether the enforcement mix shifts toward fewer, larger cases. Watch the promised annual review of the Enforcement Manual. For practitioners, the practical work is unglamorous: pick two guardrail obligations, pull the evidence, and confirm the control operated on the dates it claims.
