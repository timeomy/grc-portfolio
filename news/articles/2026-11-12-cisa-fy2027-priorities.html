CISA Wrote Its Priorities Down. The Hard Part Is Using Them

An internal memo sets CISA's fiscal 2027 focus on systemic risk and tiered support for the most critical infrastructure.

Source: Cybersecurity Dive

What happened

Nick Andersen, CISA's acting director, sent agency leaders a memo on November 2 that sets out the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency's goals for fiscal 2027. Cybersecurity Dive obtained the memo, which Nextgov first reported.

The memo names three top level priorities: leading national cyber defense, prioritizing the security of key infrastructure, and overseeing nationwide resilience. Underneath sit six "operational focus areas", six "critical information requirements", seven "priority intelligence requirements" and thirteen "mission sustainment and resilience requirements". The document then drills into the questions staff should be able to answer, including five on changes in the national risk environment and eleven on adversary plans to compromise critical networks.

Two themes run through it. The first is risky interdependencies, meaning the agency wants to understand where disruption to one system cascades into others. The second is triage. CISA should aim its finite support at "the infrastructure, systems, assets, and functions where disruption would create the greatest national consequences".

Andersen's stated purpose is to break down silos between operating divisions so that separate teams stop collecting the same information differently and assessing risk on their own terms. "The intent is to create an organization in which personnel at every level understand where we are focused, what we need to know, why that information matters, and when it must be elevated," he wrote.

Context matters. The agency has absorbed staff cuts and mission reductions, is entering a third year without a Senate confirmed director, and has narrowed the services it offers. A Palantir executive was nominated to lead CISA this week, and that nomination may face difficulty in the Senate. Andersen has been acting since February.

Why this is a GRC story

A priority list is a risk register with a budget attached. Most organizations publish a strategy and leave the hard part undone: deciding what they will not do. This memo does the opposite. It tells staff which organizations get help first, which is the same decision every security leader faces when the request queue is longer than the team.

Information requirements are the interesting part. Anyone can write three priorities. Naming seven intelligence requirements and thirteen readiness requirements forces the agency to admit what it currently cannot see, including staffing levels and workforce competencies. That is an honest gap assessment in public, and it is the section worth copying.

Partnerships are a control, and they have been strained. Reduction in services and footprint has cost the agency goodwill with the exact operators it now says it will prioritize. Rebuilding that trust is an operational task with owners and dates, not a communications exercise.

What to watch

Watch whether the tiered support model gets published in a form regulated entities can use. If the most systemically important organizations receive the most help, every other operator needs to know where it sits. Watch also whether the silo language turns into a single intake process for incident reporting, because duplicative reporting is the friction that quietly kills participation. For practitioners, the practical takeaway is smaller and more useful: write down the six things your own function must know, then check whether anyone actually collects them.
