Stolen AI Keys, $740,000 in Charges, and Controls That Arrived Late

Railway says attackers decrypted customer AI provider keys in an October breach, and it will absorb about $740,000 in unauthorized usage.

Source: The Hacker News

What happened

Railway, a platform that builds and runs applications for customers and stores their secrets as environment variables, published the full account of an intrusion that began on October 20. The attacker probed the build infrastructure, exploited a previously unknown flaw to partially escape filesystem isolation, and took a credential that build servers used to authenticate to internal systems.

That credential should only have allowed image pushes. Railway's internal task scheduler accepted it as a trusted client instead, which let the attacker read stored histories of other builds, including the variables and secrets passed to them. From there they obtained a copy of the internal codebase and found a second way in: an erroneously defined OIDC binding in one cloud environment, which yielded short lived cloud credentials.

Database logs show the attacker filtered rows by regular expression to find AI provider keys. Railway says at most 1.85 percent of workspaces, roughly 100,000, could have had any variable decrypted, and that the figure is an upper bound. Linked account tokens for 1,852 users, including bring your own key agent credentials and GitHub OAuth tokens, were also exposed and reset. The attacker read stored histories of 353 builds. Railway evicted them by 09:24 UTC on October 21.

Unauthorized spending on the stolen keys is estimated at about $740,000. Railway is working with providers to refund it and says it will cover the losses itself where a provider will not. It also filed a complaint with the FBI's IC3 and is sharing indicators with customers that have their own reporting obligations.

Why this is a GRC story

The intrusion was technical. The exposure was a permissions decision. A build credential with image push rights was trusted by a scheduler it had no business talking to. That is a service account review finding, the kind that sits in a spreadsheet for a year because nothing appears broken.

OIDC trust policies are identity controls and they need owners. A misconfigured binding in a cloud environment is a configuration error with the blast radius of a standing credential. Very few organizations test those bindings after setup, and almost none monitor them.

AI keys became the target because they are easy to monetize. Railway's forensics point to credentials being passed to resale operations for model distillation. That reframes AI provider keys as financial instruments, which means budgets, usage anomaly alerts, and per key spend caps are detective controls, not accountancy.

Someone has to own the third party cost question. When stolen keys burn $740,000 at a provider, the contract decides who pays. Railway chose to backstop it, which is decent behavior and also a reminder that most customers never checked what their own agreement says.

What to watch

Watch whether other platforms that hold AI keys publish comparably detailed reports, because the pattern here was not unique. Watch how insurers and auditors treat "unauthorized consumption" claims, since that is a loss category most policies did not anticipate. For teams running agents in production, the practical work is unglamorous: inventory every stored secret, scope it to one purpose, cap its spend, and prove that revocation takes minutes rather than a maintenance window.
